7 Best Cancellation Email Templates (2026)

Most subscription businesses send one or two cancellation emails.

We read hundreds of SaaS emails and found that the cancellation sequence has at least five moments worth an email:

  • Cancellation confirmation
  • About to lapse
  • Charge fails
  • Access ends
  • Data purge

The seven below run in that order. Some moments carry more than one email. Each entry covers when it fires, the technique that makes it work, and copy you can adapt.

1. TunnelBear: “Thanks for tunneling! You won’t be charged anymore.”

Fires: Immediately on cancellation.

A cancellation confirmation works best when it does one job. This is what doing only that job looks like. It confirms no further charges, names the date access continues to, states plainly what the account becomes afterwards, then asks how the experience was.

No save offer. No “are you sure?” No referral pitch bolted to the bottom.

Here’s the catch. That restraint is right for the confirmation, not for the flow. An offer works before the decision is final, not after it. If the only place you make one is the confirmation email, you are making it to someone who has already decided twice.

Try this:

This is to confirm you won’t receive any more charges from {{Product}}.

You’ll keep {{CurrentState}} until {{Date}}. After that, your account becomes {{PostCancellationState}}.

How was your experience? If there’s something we could have done better, we’d like to hear it. Just reply.

2. Netflix: “We’re sorry to say goodbye”

Fires: Immediately on cancellation.

Confirms the cancellation, then offers one line of save language and stops. “Obviously we’d love to have you back.”

Most confirmations do one of two things. They say nothing, or they start negotiating. One line signals you would take the customer back without suggesting the decision is still open. That is the only posture in this email that does not read as pressure, and pressure at this moment is what produces the support ticket rather than the reactivation.

Try this:

Subject: We’re sorry to say goodbye

Hi {{FirstName}},

As you requested, we’ve cancelled your {{subscription}} effective {{DayOfWeek}}, {{Date}}.

Obviously we’d love to have you back. If you change your mind, just restart it. Everything’s where you left it.

Restart {{subscription}}

3. YouTube: “You’ve cancelled YouTube Premium – and will lose your member benefits.”

Fires: Right after the confirmation, while benefits are still active.

Every benefit is paired with its consequence in the customer’s own experience. No more ad-free means ads return. No more downloads means nothing works offline. No more background play means audio stops when the screen locks.

It also asks: “Didn’t mean to cancel?” Mis-clicks and expired-card confusion land in the same inbox as customers who meant it. One line separates them.

Try this:

You’ve cancelled {{PlanName}}. Starting {{Date}}, here’s what changes:

  • {{Feature1}}. {{WhatTheyWillExperience}}.
  • {{Feature2}}. {{WhatTheyWillExperience}}.
  • {{Feature3}}. {{WhatTheyWillExperience}}.

Didn’t mean to cancel? Renew now.

Write the right-hand column in the second person and in the present tense. “Reporting is limited to 30 days” is a spec. “You’ll stop being able to pull last quarter’s numbers” is a loss.

4. Zapier: “Your Zapier Premium trial ends tomorrow”

Fires: One day before a subscription or trial lapses.

It opens with the customer’s own usage number, how many Tasks they’ve automated since signing up, before it mentions the deadline. Then it names exactly what the free plan drops them to, in numbers rather than adjectives.

Try this:

Since you started, you’ve {{UsageStat}}.

Your trial ends {{Date}}. After that you’ll drop to {{SpecificFreeLimits}}.

Keep your plan

Use it on cancellations too. Zapier runs this on trial expiration. The same logic works in a cancellation confirmation, and almost nobody puts it there:

Your subscription is canceled. You won’t be charged again.

Since {{SignupDate}}, you’ve {{UsageStat}}. All of it stays where it is until {{RetentionDate}}, so if you come back, nothing needs rebuilding.

5. Benchmark: “Don’t worry … your data is safe”

Fires: When a renewal charge fails.

The subject line does the work. Not “payment failed,” not “action required,” not “your account is suspended.” It opens by answering the question the customer will actually have, which is whether they’ve lost anything. Then it explains the billing error and walks through the fix step by step, with separate instructions for credit cards and other payment methods.

Involuntary churn is people who wanted to stay. Writing to them as though they’ve done something wrong is how you turn a billing hiccup into a cancellation.

Worth noting where this sits. A failed charge is the second chance, not the first. The card’s expiry date was in your billing system months earlier.

Try this:

Subject: Don’t worry, your {{data/account}} is safe

We tried to renew your account and hit a billing error. Nothing has been deleted and nothing is lost.

Here’s how to fix it: {{Step1}} · {{Step2}} · {{Step3}}

Update billing. We’ll renew as soon as it saves.

6. NordVPN: “Your account has expired”

Fires: The day access actually stops.

Most sequences skip this moment entirely. There’s a confirmation when the customer cancels, then silence until a winback shows up a month later. But the day access ends is the one day the loss is concrete rather than theoretical, and it’s the only day the customer can feel the product’s absence in real time.

NordVPN’s lever is the risk that returns the moment the subscription stops. Browsing without a VPN leaves you exposed. If your product’s absence creates something the customer will notice today, name it.

Try this:

Subject: Your account has expired

Your {{Product}} subscription ended today.

{{WhatIsNowTrueForThem}}. {{ConcreteConsequenceStartingNow}}.

Renew and pick up where you left off

Send it on the day, not the day after. The whole value of this email is that it arrives while the customer is noticing.

7. Other Goose: “Important: Your data is being deleted.”

Fires: On a scheduled purge of accounts inactive due to cancellation.

This is usually the last email a churned customer gets. The deadline is real and the loss is permanent, which is why it tends to outperform softer reactivation attempts sent weeks earlier.

It names the exact removal date, states plainly which account states trigger it, and explains that the deletion is a deliberate privacy practice rather than a punishment.

The reason it works is that it isn’t a winback email. It’s a notice with a genuine deadline, and a reactivation link happens to be the fastest way to stop the clock.

Try this:

We periodically remove data for accounts inactive due to cancellation, failed payment, or closure.

Your data is scheduled for removal on {{Date}}. That includes {{SpecificExamples}}.

Export everything · Reactivate and keep it

No hard feelings either way. This is the last email you’ll get from us.
